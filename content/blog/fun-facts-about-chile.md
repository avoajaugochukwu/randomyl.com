> Chile is a long, skinny country on South America's Pacific coast that stretches about 4,300 km (2,670 mi) north to south while averaging only around 175 km wide. It holds the world's driest non-polar desert, the oldest known mummies, Easter Island's moai, and a record-setting pool. Here are 35 facts worth sharing.

Chile looks like someone took a country and stretched it. It runs down nearly the entire western edge of South America, from the bone-dry north all the way to the icy fjords near Antarctica. That shape alone gives Chile a wild range of landscapes, and the facts below cover its geography, food, history, and a few records that are genuinely hard to believe.

## Geography and extremes

Chile's geography is where most of its "wait, really?" facts live. The combination of a giant ocean, the Andes, and one very dry desert makes for some serious extremes.

### 1. It's one of the longest countries on Earth

Chile stretches roughly 4,300 km (2,670 mi) from north to south, while averaging only about 175 km (109 mi) east to west, according to [Wikipedia's overview of Chile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chile). That long-and-narrow shape is one of the most distinctive national outlines anywhere.

### 2. The Atacama is the driest non-polar desert in the world

Northern Chile's Atacama Desert is described as "the driest non-polar desert in the world and the largest fog desert on Earth," with average annual rainfall of only around 15 mm (0.6 in), per [Wikipedia's Atacama Desert article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atacama_Desert). Some weather stations there have never recorded measurable rain.

### 3. Parts of it may have gone centuries without rain

Evidence suggests some areas of the Atacama saw no significant rainfall for roughly 400 years, between 1570 and 1971, [according to that same Atacama record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atacama_Desert). That is about as close to "never rains" as a place on land gets.

### 4. It's so dry because of a double rain shadow

The Atacama sits between two mountain ranges, the Andes and the Chilean Coast Range, which block moisture from both the Pacific and the Atlantic. The cool Humboldt Current offshore adds to the drying effect, creating what the [Atacama entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atacama_Desert) calls a "two-sided rain shadow."

### 5. NASA tests Mars instruments there

Because the Atacama's soil and near-total lack of microbial life resemble Martian conditions, NASA uses it as a Mars analog. A 2003 study even ran Viking-style life-detection tests on Atacama soil near Yungay and found no life, as noted in the [Atacama Desert overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atacama_Desert).

### 6. Some of the world's biggest telescopes live in the Atacama

The desert's dry, clear skies make it a stargazer's dream. The European Southern Observatory runs major facilities there, including Paranal (home to the Very Large Telescope) and the ALMA array on the Chajnantor plateau, per [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atacama_Desert). The even larger Extremely Large Telescope is under construction nearby.

### 7. The Andes run almost the whole length of the country

The Andes form Chile's long eastern spine, separating it from Argentina and Bolivia. The mountains feed the country's rivers and shape nearly everything about its climate.

### 8. Chile claims a slice of Antarctica

Chile claims about 1,250,000 square km (480,000 sq mi) of Antarctic territory, as listed in [Wikipedia's Chile profile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chile). It's one of several nations with overlapping claims on the frozen continent.

### 9. It has thousands of kilometers of Pacific coastline

That long shape gives Chile an enormous coastline along the Pacific, which keeps the cool Humboldt Current, rich fisheries, and a strong seafood culture front and center in daily life.

### 10. Patagonia covers the wild, windy south

Far from the desert north, southern Chile turns into Patagonia: glaciers, fjords, and granite towers. Torres del Paine National Park is the postcard image, full of turquoise lakes and dramatic peaks.

### 11. Home to the world's smallest deer

The pudú, native to southern Chile and Argentina, is the smallest deer species on Earth, standing only a little taller than a house cat, as covered by the [BBC Science Focus](https://www.sciencefocus.com/nature/pudu-worlds-smallest-deer). It hides in the temperate forests of the south.

### 12. One island hosts two penguin species together

Puñihuil, a set of small islands off Chiloé in the northwest, is described as the only place in the world where both Magellanic and Humboldt penguins gather to breed side by side.

### 13. The monkey puzzle tree is a living fossil

The araucaria, or monkey puzzle tree (*Araucaria araucana*), is native to central and southern Chile and can grow 30–40 m (98–131 ft) tall, per [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Araucaria_araucana). Its spiky, prehistoric look has earned it protected status.

## Culture, food, and symbols

Chileans have a saying that their country is a *loco geografía* of contrasts, and the culture matches: poetry, hearty food, and symbols pulled straight from the landscape.

### 14. Chile produced two Nobel Prize-winning poets

Gabriela Mistral won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1945, becoming the first Latin American to do so, and Pablo Neruda followed in 1971, [according to Marca Chile](https://www.marcachile.cl/en/gabriela-mistral-and-pablo-neruda-the-chilean-nobel-prize-winners/). For a country its size, that's a remarkable literary record.

### 15. Mistral once taught the teenage Neruda

In a small-world twist, Gabriela Mistral was a head teacher in Temuco when a teenage poet named Neftalí Reyes showed her his work. He later took the pen name Pablo Neruda, per [Marca Chile](https://www.marcachile.cl/en/gabriela-mistral-and-pablo-neruda-the-chilean-nobel-prize-winners/).

### 16. Chileans are among the world's top bread eaters

Chile is second only to Germany in total bread consumption, a fact echoed across food-culture coverage like [Matador Network](https://matadornetwork.com/read/chile-food-culture/). Marraqueta and hallulla rolls are daily staples.

### 17. The completo is a hot dog turned into a meal

Chile's beloved *completo* is a hot dog piled with chopped tomato, mashed avocado, sauerkraut, and a generous swirl of mayonnaise. The *completo italiano* even mirrors the green-white-red of the Italian flag, as the [Completo entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completo) explains.

### 18. The pisco sour is the national drink

The tangy, frothy pisco sour is Chile's national cocktail, made from grape brandy, lime juice, and sugar, [per Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisco_sour). Both Chile and Peru proudly claim it, which makes for a friendly cross-border rivalry.

### 19. Empanadas are everywhere

The baked *empanada de pino*, stuffed with seasoned beef, onion, a slice of egg, and an olive, is a national comfort food, especially around the September independence celebrations.

### 20. The flag's colors tell a story

Chile's flag uses a blue square with a white star over white and red bands. The blue stands for the sky, white for the snow-capped Andes, and red for the blood spilled in the fight for independence, as described in [World Atlas](https://www.worldatlas.com/flags/chile).

### 21. Its coat of arms has a condor and a deer

The national coat of arms, dating to 1834, is supported by an Andean condor and a huemul, a deer endemic to the region, [according to Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coat_of_arms_of_Chile). Both wear golden naval crowns honoring the Chilean Navy.

### 22. The huemul is the rare national animal

The huemul, or South Andean deer, is endangered and seldom seen. Spotting one in Torres del Paine is treated as a once-in-a-blue-moon thrill by wildlife guides like [Natural World Safaris](https://www.naturalworldsafaris.com/latin-america/chile/wildlife).

## History and ancient roots

Chile's human story runs deep, from desert cultures that mummified their dead to record-breaking earthquakes that reshaped the coast.

### 23. The world's oldest known mummies are Chilean

The Chinchorro people of the Atacama coast were artificially mummifying their dead by around 5050 BC, roughly 2,000 years before the Egyptians, according to [Wikipedia's Chinchorro mummies article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinchorro_mummies). They are the oldest examples of intentional mummification known.

### 24. Everyone got mummified, not just the elite

Unlike Egypt, where mummification was mostly for royalty, the Chinchorro mummified people of all ages and statuses, including infants. The practice is detailed in the [Chinchorro mummies entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinchorro_mummies).

### 25. Those mummies are now a UNESCO site

In 2021, the Chinchorro settlements and mummification sites in the Arica and Parinacota region were declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site, as the [Chinchorro record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinchorro_mummies) notes.

### 26. Chile recorded the strongest earthquake ever measured

The 1960 Valdivia earthquake in southern Chile reached magnitude 9.5, the most powerful quake ever recorded by instruments. It triggered tsunamis that crossed the Pacific to Hawaii, Japan, and the Philippines.

### 27. Independence was declared in 1818

Chile formally declared independence from Spain on 12 February 1818, after a national government junta first formed on 18 September 1810, per [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chile). The September date is still celebrated as the country's main patriotic holiday.

### 28. Easter Island became part of Chile in 1888

Chile annexed Easter Island, known as Rapa Nui, in 1888, [according to Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chile). It remains one of the most remote inhabited places on the planet.

### 29. The capital is Santiago

Santiago, set in a valley between the Andes and the coast, is Chile's capital and largest city, [per Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chile). On clear days the snowy Andes loom right over the skyline.

## Surprising records and oddities

Some Chile facts feel almost made up. These are the ones most likely to stop a conversation.

### 30. Easter Island's moai are massive and mysterious

Rapa Nui's famous moai statues number 887 and can reach about 12 m (40 ft) tall and weigh up to 75 tons, according to [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/715/). The Polynesian Rapa Nui people carved them centuries ago, and how they moved them still fascinates researchers.

### 31. Easter Island is wildly remote

Rapa Nui sits roughly 3,700 km (about 2,300 mi) from mainland Chile, making it one of the most isolated inhabited islands on Earth. It marks one corner of the Polynesian Triangle.

### 32. Chile had the world's largest swimming pool

The San Alfonso del Mar resort in Algarrobo built a seawater pool about 1,013 m (3,323 ft) long covering 8 hectares (20 acres), which entered the Guinness Book of World Records as the world's largest pool when it opened in 2006, [per Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Alfonso_del_Mar). Guests have actually sailed boats on it.

### 33. It's the world's top copper producer

Chile is the world's largest producer and exporter of copper, and its Escondida mine is the highest-producing copper mine on the planet, [according to Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minera_Escondida). Copper has long been the backbone of the national economy.

### 34. Chilean wine is a global heavyweight

Chile's Central Valley gives the country a high world ranking in wine exports, as [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chile/Mineral-resources-noncarboniferous) notes. The dry climate and natural barriers also helped Chile dodge some grape diseases that hit European vineyards.

### 35. The country is a volcano hotspot

Sitting on the Pacific Ring of Fire, Chile has well over 100 volcanoes, many of them active. That volcanic activity is the flip side of the same forces that produce its frequent earthquakes.

Chile packs an outrageous amount into one narrow strip of land: the driest desert on Earth, the oldest mummies ever found, record-breaking quakes, a record-breaking pool, two Nobel poets, and the silent stone faces of Easter Island. It's the kind of country that rewards curiosity, because every region feels like a different world.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Chile really the longest country in the world?

Chile is consistently described as one of the longest countries by its north-south span, stretching about 4,300 km (2,670 mi) while staying very narrow, around 175 km wide on average, [per Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chile). Whether it's "the" single longest depends on exactly how you measure, but its long, skinny shape is one of the most extreme on any map.

### Why is the Atacama Desert so dry?

The Atacama sits in a double rain shadow between the Andes and the Chilean Coast Range, which block moisture from both oceans, while the cool Humboldt Current offshore reduces evaporation that would otherwise feed rain clouds. The result, [explained on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atacama_Desert), is the driest non-polar desert on Earth, with some spots that have never recorded measurable rain.

### Are the Chinchorro mummies older than Egypt's?

Yes. The oldest intentionally preserved Chinchorro mummy dates to around 5050 BC, while the earliest Egyptian mummies are roughly 2,000 years younger, according to [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinchorro_mummies). That makes the Chinchorro the oldest known examples of artificial mummification in the world.

### Is Easter Island part of Chile?

Yes. Easter Island, called Rapa Nui by its Polynesian inhabitants, has been part of Chile since 1888 and lies roughly 3,700 km off the mainland in the Pacific. It's famous for its 887 moai statues and its status as one of the most remote inhabited islands on Earth.
